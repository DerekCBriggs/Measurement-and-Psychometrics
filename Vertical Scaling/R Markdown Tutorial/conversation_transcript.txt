DEREK:
Why do you think the concurrent calibration approach does better at
estimating the true SD relative to the separate calibration approach?

CLAUDE:
The difference comes down to how the SD is being estimated in each
approach.

In separate calibration, the SD we report is sd(eap_scores) — the
standard deviation of the EAP point estimates. EAP scores are posterior
means, and posterior means are always shrunken toward the population mean
relative to the true abilities. This is the classic regression-to-the-mean
effect: students with extreme true abilities get pulled back toward zero.
The spread of the point estimates is always less than the spread of the
true abilities, so sd(eap_scores) systematically underestimates the true
SD. The chain linking constants shift the means correctly, but they don't
fix this shrinkage.

In concurrent calibration, the SD is estimated as a structural model
parameter — it's the sigma of the population distribution for each group,
estimated directly via the EM algorithm. During the M-step, the variance
estimate accounts for both the spread of the posterior means and the
posterior uncertainty around each person's score. In other words, it
"knows" that the EAP scores are shrunken and corrects for it. So it's
estimating the variance of the latent distribution itself, not the
variance of a set of shrunken point estimates.

That's the fundamental distinction: separate calibration derives the SD
from the spread of point estimates (which are attenuated), while
concurrent calibration estimates it as a parameter of the generating
distribution (which is not).
